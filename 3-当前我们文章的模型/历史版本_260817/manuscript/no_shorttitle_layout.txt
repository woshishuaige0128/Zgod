1    1. Introduction

 2   2. Structural modeling and condensation formulations

 3      In a multi-degree-of-freedom (MDOF) real-time hybrid simulation (RTHS), the physical
 4   substructure often contains more interface coordinates than the transfer system can impose inde-
 5   pendently. Model reduction is adopted to express the coordinates without independent actuation
 6   through a reduced set of experimentally imposed coordinates. In this section, the structural
 7   modeling framework and the condensation formulations are established. The full-order equation of
 8   motion is decomposed into the contributions of the numerical and physical substructures. Guyan
 9   static condensation and Craig–Bampton dynamic condensation are then formulated on a common
10   partition of retained and condensed coordinates, and the two methods differ only in the construction
11   of the transformation matrix.

90    3. Delay-dependent stability formulation

91       In an MDOF RTHS, each actuated coordinate of the physical substructure is driven by a
92    separate actuator, and each actuator introduces its own response delay. Condensation expresses
93    the condensed coordinates through the retained coordinates, and the delays acting on the retained
94    coordinates are therefore carried by the reconstructed response of the condensed coordinates. In this
95    section, the delay-dependent stability formulation of the condensed RTHS system is established.
96    The actuator delays are represented as integer multiples of the integration time step, and their
97    transmission through the Guyan and Craig–Bampton transformations is identified. The condensed
98    substructures are then assembled into a discrete-time closed-loop model with an LQR controller,
99    and the stability is assessed from the modulus of the dominant pole of the closed loop.

100   3.1. Multiple-delay representation and delay propagation through condensation

101      The delay of an RTHS system originates from the numerical integration, the data exchange
102   between the numerical and physical substructures, the conversion between digital and analogue
103   signals, and the response of the servo-hydraulic actuator [4]. The actuator response delay is retained
104   in the present formulation and the other three sources are excluded, which isolates the interaction
105   between the delay and the model reduction.
106      The actuator delay is taken as an integer multiple of the integration time step [5] [DOI
107   10.1002/eqe.2467]. The delay then becomes a power of 𝑧 after the 𝑧 transform, which avoids

233   4.2. Substructure division and selection of retained DOFs

234      The position of the boundary between the physical and the numerical substructures affects both
235   the dynamic response and the accuracy of the condensation. Two divisions are compared and both
236   are shown in Fig. 4. In each of them the outer bay of the frame is implemented as the physical
237   substructure and the remaining part forms the numerical substructure. The outer bay is selected
238   because a division at an inner bay would split the numerical substructure into two separate parts.
239      In division I the physical substructure covers the first two storeys of the outer bay together with
240   the connected beams and columns. The lower storeys carry the largest storey shear of the first two
241   modes, and the interface is close to the actuator locations, which shortens the loading path. The
242   physical substructure holds about 40% of the DOFs of the whole structure.
243      In division II the physical substructure covers all three storeys of the outer bay. More DOFs are
244   kept at the interface, which represents the modal coupling between the storeys more completely,
245   and the physical substructure holds about 60% of the DOFs of the whole structure.
246      Three requirements govern the selection of the retained coordinates. The coordinates carrying
247   the dominant response remain explicit, the condensed response is recoverable through the relations
248   of Section 2, and the reduction is large enough to be of practical use. The horizontal storey
249   displacements govern the global response of a frame under a base excitation and carry most of
250   the energy of the low-order modes. The nodal rotations mainly represent local bending and are
251   coupled to the horizontal displacements through the off-diagonal terms of the stiffness matrix, and
252   they are condensed accordingly. Condensing all rotations would shift the higher modes, and several
253   rotations of the numerical substructure are therefore kept.
254      Both divisions are limited to two independent actuation channels. Division I directly actuates
255   both horizontal coordinates of its physical substructure. Division II has three horizontal coordinates
256   under the same two-channel constraint, and the middle-storey coordinate 𝜓6 is therefore condensed

257   and reconstructed from the retained coordinates. Division II is the more demanding case, since a
258   coordinate carrying a substantial share of the storey response is no longer imposed directly. The
259   retained horizontal coordinates of the physical substructure are the coordinates imposed by the
260   two actuators in both divisions. The retained set and the actuated set thus coincide, and the same
261   assignment is adopted in the accuracy analysis and in the stability analysis.

344   5. Results and discussion

345      The two condensed models of each division are compared with the full-order reference model of

457      Table 5 is the quantitative counterpart of the mechanism of Section 3.1. A large modal
458   redistribution ratio corresponds to a large share of the modal energy originally distributed over
459   the condensed coordinates moved onto the retained coordinates. The retained coordinates of the
460   physical substructure are the coordinates driven by the actuators, and the same quantity therefore
461   measures the response of the reduced model carried by delayed channels. The ratio is obtained
462   from the modal solutions of the full-order and of the condensed model, and it is available as soon
463   as the condensed model is built, ahead of any closed-loop delay scan.
464      The two models with a modal redistribution ratio above 0.3 are the two models whose stability
465   domain contracts most, and both are Guyan models. The ratio ranks candidate condensed models
466   ahead of the delay scan, and the four models of Table 5 support a ranking rather than a general
467   threshold.
468      The accuracy and the stability results together indicate the range of application of each method.
469   Craig–Bampton condensation keeps the frequency error below 1% and the NRMSE below 0.06%
470   in every case examined, and its stability boundary is the closer of the two to that of the unreduced
471   model. It is therefore the method for a condensed principal coordinate and for an excitation carrying
472   energy near or above the fundamental frequency. Guyan condensation produces the smallest
473   reduced model, at six generalized coordinates against twelve for Craig–Bampton. In division I
474   its second natural frequency is in error by 5.411% and its NRMSE reaches 1.995% in the band
475   around the fundamental frequency, which sets the tolerance required of the application. In division
476   II its frequency error reaches 24.575% and its NRMSE reaches 19.059%, more than an order of
477   magnitude above the Craig–Bampton values, and it also gives the smallest stability domain.
478      The three indices do not carry equal weight in this judgement. The frequency error and the
479   NRMSE of the Guyan model of division II are the largest values of Tables 3 and 4, whereas the
480   MAC of the same model remains above 0.92. A modal correlation index evaluated on the retained
481   coordinates alone is therefore insufficient to accept a condensed model for RTHS, and it is applied
482   together with a frequency error and a time-domain error.
